How to Write a Resume That Gets Noticed

InfoNest Editorial
July 25, 2026
8 min read

The hiring funnel is brutal. For competitive roles at larger companies, a resume might compete with hundreds of others, and most are filtered out before a human ever reads them. Understanding how that filtering works, and what recruiters actually look for in the 6-10 seconds they spend on a resume, changes how you approach the document entirely.

Understanding Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS)

Most companies above a certain size use Applicant Tracking Systems, software that parses, scores, and filters resumes before routing them to human reviewers. ATS systems match your resume against the job description, looking for keyword matches, experience indicators, and formatting they can parse correctly.

The practical implications: use standard section headings (Experience, Education, Skills), avoid tables, columns, headers and footers, and graphics, many ATS systems can't parse these correctly. More importantly, tailor your resume for each application by reviewing the job description and incorporating relevant keywords and phrases that appear in it. Not to manipulate the system through keyword stuffing, but because a well-tailored resume genuinely reflects the relevance of your experience to the specific role.

The Core Principle: Communicate Value, Not Duties

The most common resume mistake is describing job duties rather than communicating impact. "Responsible for managing social media accounts" tells a hiring manager what you did. "Grew Instagram following from 4,000 to 22,000 over 8 months and drove $40k in e-commerce revenue through organic content" tells them what you achieved. The second version is dramatically more compelling.

For every role, ask: what changed because of what I did? What did I build, improve, save, grow, or prevent? Quantify where possible. Even estimates are better than no numbers at all, "reduced onboarding time by approximately 30%" is more concrete than "streamlined onboarding process."

The Structure That Works

For most professionals with experience, a single-page or two-page reverse-chronological resume is the standard and most readable format. The sections, in order:

  • Contact information: Name, city/country (not full address), email, LinkedIn URL, and if relevant, a portfolio or GitHub link. No photo in the US/UK, it can introduce bias and some ATS systems can't parse it.
  • Professional summary (optional): 2-3 sentences summarizing who you are professionally and what you bring to the role. Useful for senior candidates or career changers; skip it if you're early in your career and don't have much to say yet.
  • Experience: Reverse chronological. Company, title, dates (month and year), and 3-5 bullet points per role focused on achievements.
  • Skills: A concise list of relevant technical skills, tools, and languages. Put this near the top if you're in a technical field.
  • Education: Degree, institution, graduation year. Unless you're a recent graduate, this goes at the bottom, experience is what matters most.

Writing Bullet Points That Work

Each bullet point should follow a structure: action verb + task + result. Start with a strong, specific verb (not "worked on" or "helped with"). Describe what you did. Quantify or describe the outcome. Examples:

  • ✅ "Redesigned client onboarding workflow, reducing average time-to-first-value from 14 days to 6 days"
  • ✅ "Led migration of 3 legacy PHP applications to Node.js, improving page load times by 60% and reducing server costs by $18k annually"
  • ❌ "Worked on website improvement projects"
  • ❌ "Responsible for various coding tasks"

Tailoring for Every Application

A generic resume sent to 100 companies will perform worse than a tailored resume sent to 20. Review the job description carefully. What skills are listed most prominently? What does the role's context suggest about what this company values? Adjust your skills section, reorder bullet points to lead with the most relevant experience, and if you use a summary, write it specifically for this role.

This sounds like a lot of work per application. It is, and it pays off. Response rates from tailored applications are consistently higher than from generic ones.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using an objective statement ("I'm a motivated professional seeking an opportunity") instead of a summary or no summary at all
  • Including references or the phrase "references available upon request", everyone knows you'll provide references; no need to waste space
  • Including personal details irrelevant to the role (marital status, age, hobbies that don't demonstrate relevant skills)
  • Using dense blocks of text, recruiters skim; bullet points win
  • Using a font smaller than 10pt or margins thinner than 0.5 inches
  • Submitting as a Word doc when PDF is available, PDF preserves formatting across different systems
