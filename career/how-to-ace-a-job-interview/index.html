How to Ace a Job Interview: Preparation, Practice and Presence

The candidate who gets the job is rarely the most qualified person in the applicant pool. It's usually the person who prepared most deliberately and communicated their value most clearly. Interviews are a learnable skill, and most people significantly underprepare for them.

Research That Goes Beyond the Website

Reading a company's "About" page is table stakes. Interviewers can tell immediately when someone's preparation went no further than a quick browse of the marketing copy. Genuine preparation looks different. It means reading recent news coverage and press releases to understand what the company is currently prioritizing. It means reading recent earnings calls or reports if the company is public. It means looking at Glassdoor and LinkedIn reviews to understand the culture from employees' perspectives, and checking the job posting against the company's stated values to anticipate what specific concerns they're trying to solve by hiring for this role.

This level of research serves two purposes. First, it allows you to tailor your answers to what the company actually cares about rather than giving generic responses. Second, it demonstrates genuine interest, which interviewers consistently rate as one of the most important qualities they look for, because enthusiasm that is backed by knowledge feels authentic where surface-level enthusiasm does not.

The STAR Method for Behavioral Questions

Behavioral interview questions, those that begin with "Tell me about a time when..." or "Give me an example of...", are among the most common and most poorly handled interview questions. The STAR method gives your answers a structure that is both complete and easy to follow.

STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, and Result. You open with enough context to make the situation clear, explain specifically what you were responsible for, walk through the actions you took and why, and close with the concrete outcome, ideally quantified. The mistake most candidates make is spending too much time on the Situation and not enough on the Action and Result, which are the parts the interviewer is actually evaluating.

Prepare at least six to eight strong stories from your career before your interview. Each should demonstrate a different quality: problem-solving, leadership, handling conflict, managing under pressure, learning from failure, and working across teams. Once you have these stories ready, they can be adapted to answer a wide range of behavioral questions.

Behavioral Questions to Prepare For

  • Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager. How did you handle it?
  • Describe a situation where you had to meet a tight deadline. What did you do?
  • Give me an example of a time you failed. What did you learn from it?
  • Tell me about a time you had to influence someone without direct authority.
  • Describe the most complex project you have managed. How did you approach it?

Common Questions You Should Have Answers For

"Tell me about yourself" is the opening question in most interviews, and it's the one most people handle least well. A strong answer is not a resume recitation. It is a two-minute narrative that connects your past experience to your current direction and explains why this particular role is the logical next step. It should feel natural rather than rehearsed, which means you need to practice it enough that the structure is automatic even though the specific words vary.

"Why do you want to work here?" requires that research work to pay off. Your answer should reference something specific about this company, not something that could apply to any company in the industry. "I've been following your expansion into Southeast Asia and I'm specifically interested in the supply chain challenges you're navigating" is a real answer. "I'm excited by the company's innovative culture and growth trajectory" is a non-answer that signals you haven't done your homework.

"What are your weaknesses?" remains a staple despite being widely considered a poor interview question. The best approach is to choose a genuine weakness that is real enough to be credible, not a disguised strength, but one that is not central to the role, and pair it with a specific thing you are doing to address it.

Questions to Ask Them

The questions you ask at the end of an interview are as important as the answers you give. Asking no questions suggests disengagement. Asking questions about salary or benefits in an early-stage interview suggests misplaced priorities. The best questions demonstrate that you have thought seriously about the role and the company.

Strong questions might include: What does success look like in this role in the first 90 days? What are the biggest challenges the person in this role will face? How do you see this team evolving over the next two years? What do you personally find most rewarding about working here? These questions demonstrate genuine curiosity and often produce valuable information about whether the role is actually right for you.

Managing Interview Nerves

Almost everyone experiences some level of anxiety before and during interviews, including highly experienced candidates. Moderate anxiety is actually helpful, it sharpens attention and increases energy. The problem is when anxiety becomes so intense that it disrupts thinking and communication.

The most effective interventions are preparation itself, because familiarity reduces uncertainty which is the primary source of anxiety, and reframing. Research by Stanford psychologist Alison Wood Brooks found that telling yourself "I'm excited" rather than "I'm calm" before a high-stakes performance improves outcomes, because excitement and anxiety involve similar physiological states and excitement is easier to access.

On a practical level, arriving early or logging in ahead of time for a remote interview gives you time to settle. Slowing your speech pace when nervous is harder than it sounds, but worth practicing. And remembering that interviewers are almost universally rooting for candidates to succeed, because a good hire solves their problem, can shift the dynamic from adversarial to collaborative in your mind.

Specific Tips for Remote Interviews

Remote interviews have added a layer of technical and environmental variables that in-person interviews don't have. Test your camera, microphone, and internet connection the day before, not ten minutes before the call starts. Find a background that is neutral and uncluttered, or use a professional virtual background. Ensure your lighting comes from in front of you rather than behind you, otherwise you'll appear as a silhouette.

Look at the camera rather than at the interviewer's face on your screen. This feels unnatural but reads on their end as eye contact rather than distraction. Silence your phone, close unnecessary browser tabs, and put a sign on your door if you share your space with others. These details are small individually but collectively signal that you take the conversation seriously.

The Follow-Up That Gets Remembered

Sending a thank-you email within 24 hours of your interview is standard practice, but most candidates write generic messages that say little more than "Thank you for your time, I remain very interested in the position." A thank-you email that actually helps you is specific. It references something from the conversation, reinforces your fit for a specific aspect of the role, and adds one piece of information or perspective that didn't come up in the interview.

This is not manipulative. It's the same as following up any professional meeting by reinforcing the key points and keeping the relationship warm. If you interviewed with multiple people, send individual emails to each with distinct content. The small extra effort creates a disproportionate impression.
