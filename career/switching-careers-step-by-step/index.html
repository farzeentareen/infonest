Switching Careers: A Step-by-Step Approach

Alex Morgan
July 17, 2026
9 min read
🔄

Career changes are increasingly common and more achievable than most people assume. The path isn't painless, there's usually a financial dip, a period of being a beginner again, and real uncertainty. But approached systematically, a major career transition is among the most financially and personally rewarding moves an adult can make.

Step 1: Clarify What You're Moving Toward, Not Just Away From

The most common career change mistake is defining the goal by what you're leaving rather than what you're entering. "I want to escape my current job" is motivation, not a plan. Before doing anything else, clarify what specifically appeals to you about the target field: the work itself, the compensation ceiling, the working conditions, the values alignment, or something else. Without clarity here, you risk making a move that sounds better but recreates the same problems in a different context.

Conduct informational interviews, conversations with people already working in your target field. Ask what they do day to day, what they wish they'd known, what they find genuinely satisfying and genuinely frustrating. Most people are willing to spend 30 minutes sharing their experience with someone genuinely curious. These conversations are the most reliable way to test whether your assumptions about a field match reality.

Step 2: Assess the Gap and What It Requires

Once you've identified a target, map the gap between where you are and where you need to be. This includes: skills you don't have (and how to develop them), credentials that may be required (and whether they are truly required or merely conventional), experience you lack (and how to get some before leaving your current role), and network gaps (who do you need to know in the new field?).

Be honest about the gap without inflating it. People change careers across enormous skill gaps constantly. The gap is real and should be taken seriously, but it is not a reason not to proceed, it's a project plan.

Step 3: Build a Financial Runway

Most career transitions involve a temporary income reduction, either during a period of retraining, or when entering a new field at a more junior level. Planning for this is essential. Before beginning a transition, aim to have at minimum 6–12 months of living expenses saved. Some transitions (into highly competitive fields like acting or professional athletics) may require longer runways or income supplementation through side work. A clear financial picture prevents the career change from being derailed by financial pressure at a critical moment.

Step 4: Skill Build While Still Employed

Wherever possible, develop skills for your target field while still employed in your current role. This reduces the financial pressure of the transition, gives you something concrete to point to in job applications, and allows you to test your interest in the new field before fully committing. Options include: evening and weekend courses, online self-study (many excellent resources are free), freelance projects in the new field, or volunteering in contexts that develop relevant skills.

For career changes into tech specifically, software development, data science, UX design, it is very common to make the transition while employed, with 6–18 months of self-directed learning before starting to apply.

Step 5: Manage the Narrative

Your resume and interviews will need to address the career change directly. Don't apologize for it; reframe it as intentional and coherent. What skills and experiences transfer from your previous field? The account manager who moves into sales engineering brings client relationship skills and communication skills that are genuinely valuable, those should be central to the story, not treated as irrelevant.

Identify the skills, perspectives, and experiences that are genuinely transferable and lead with them. Frame the transition as adding a dimension rather than starting from zero, even if some of it does feel like starting from zero.

Step 6: Expect the Junior Stage

Most career changers entering a new field start at a more junior level than their total years of experience would suggest. This is normal and worth accepting in advance rather than resenting when it arrives. The mid-career career changer brings real advantages, professional maturity, work ethic, perspective, and usually better communication skills than a 22-year-old, but they lack domain experience, and starting roles reflect that. The junior stage in a new field typically lasts 12–24 months before skills and credibility accumulate enough to accelerate again.
