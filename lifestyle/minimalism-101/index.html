Minimalism 101: Living Better With Less

InfoNest Editorial
July 20, 2026
8 min read
🪴

Minimalism has accumulated a reputation for aesthetic extremism, all-white rooms, 100 possessions, no ornament, no warmth. The practical version, which is what most people who describe themselves as minimalists actually practice, is less dramatic and more useful: owning things intentionally, removing what doesn't serve you, and resisting the cultural pressure to accumulate by default.

What Minimalism Is and Isn't

Minimalism is not poverty or deprivation. It's not a competition to own the fewest possible things. It's not about aesthetics (though decluttered spaces tend to feel calmer). At its core, minimalism is a practice of intentionality: actively choosing what comes into your life and your space rather than accumulating by default.

The core question of minimalism, borrowed from Marie Kondo and others: does this add value to my life? Not "might I need this someday?" or "did I pay a lot for it?" or "do I feel guilty getting rid of it?", but does it genuinely add value now? That question applied consistently to physical possessions, digital files, subscriptions, commitments, and relationships is the essence of a minimalist approach.

Why People Find It Useful

The practical benefits of owning less and having less clutter are well-documented in the environmental psychology literature. Physical clutter is cognitively taxing, every object in your environment is a potential attention demand and a decision to be made. Reducing clutter reduces this cognitive overhead. Studies have found that cluttered home environments are associated with higher cortisol levels, particularly in women. The correlation is not surprising: visual complexity signals unfinished business to the brain.

There are also financial implications. Every unnecessary possession represents money spent. Habitual minimalists report that practicing intentionality about possessions naturally reduces impulse spending, when you're in the habit of asking "does this add genuine value?" before buying, more purchases get declined before being made. Over years, this produces significant savings.

Getting Started: The Practical Process

The most sustainable approach to decluttering is not the dramatic one-weekend purge (which often leads to regret, then re-accumulation) but a methodical, category-by-category process. Marie Kondo's sequence, clothes, books, papers, miscellaneous, sentimental items, is well-designed because it builds decision-making confidence in low-stakes categories before applying it to sentimental objects.

For each category: gather everything in one place, handle each item, ask whether it adds value, and remove what doesn't. "Remove" doesn't mean throw away, donate, sell, give to someone who will use it. The decision to let go is easier when you know the item has a better destination.

Applying Minimalism to Digital Life

Physical minimalism without digital minimalism addresses only part of the problem. The same principles apply to: email inboxes (unsubscribe from everything you reliably delete without reading; archive or delete what doesn't need action), digital files (periodic decluttering of downloads, desktop, documents), smartphone apps (remove anything you haven't opened in a month; keep the home screen to essentials), and subscriptions (audit regularly, most households have several forgotten recurring charges).

A Minimalist Approach to Buying

The minimalist's most useful purchasing habit is the waiting period: when you want to buy something non-essential, wait 30 days. A significant proportion of purchases you would have made in the moment will no longer seem necessary a month later, the desire has faded. For those that survive the waiting period, the purchase is more likely to be genuinely wanted rather than reflexively made.

Related practices: distinguish between wants and needs before purchasing; prefer quality over quantity (one good pair of shoes used for years produces less waste and often less total cost than several cheap pairs); and recognize that sales and deals are not savings, they're spending made to feel virtuous.

What Minimalism Is Not a Solution For

Minimalism is a tool, not a philosophy that solves everything. It won't fix a chaotic life with too many commitments (though it can help address commitments too). It won't eliminate unhappiness or provide purpose. It doesn't require living ascetically or abandoning things that genuinely bring you joy. The goal is a life where your possessions and commitments serve you, rather than you serving them, however many or few that turns out to be for you specifically.
