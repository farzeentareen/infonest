How to Choose the Right Degree Program

InfoNest Editorial
July 20, 2026
9 min read
🏛️

Choosing a university degree is one of the most significant decisions many people make, often at 17 or 18, with limited career knowledge, substantial social pressure, and enormous financial implications. The stakes warrant a more rigorous approach than most people apply.

First: Question the Assumption That a Degree Is Required

University is not the right path for everyone, and the cultural assumption that it is has caused a great deal of unnecessary debt and mismatched careers. Before committing to a degree, honestly assess whether the careers you're targeting actually require one. Software development, trades, digital marketing, entrepreneurship, and many other viable careers don't necessarily require a university degree. Research the specific career paths you're considering before assuming formal education is the entry requirement.

Understand What You're Actually Buying

A university degree provides several different things, and it's worth being clear about which ones you're actually paying for: subject knowledge and skills (varies enormously by program and institution), credential signaling (tells employers you completed something difficult), a professional network (peers, professors, alumni), and time and structure for personal development. For some fields, medicine, law, engineering, academia, the credential is necessary and the subject knowledge is directly applied. For others, the credential matters primarily as a signal, and the actual skills are built elsewhere. Knowing which category your intended field falls into changes how you should evaluate programs.

Questions to Ask Before Choosing a Program

Employment outcomes: What percentage of graduates in this program are employed in their field within 6–12 months of graduation? At what salary? Most universities publish this data; if yours doesn't, that's a red flag. Check industry salary surveys and job postings to understand realistic compensation in your target field.

Return on investment: Calculate the total cost of the degree (tuition × years + living costs + opportunity cost of income foregone) against the realistic salary trajectory in your intended field. Some degrees generate exceptional ROI. Others produce graduates who spend a decade repaying debt on a salary that never justifies the investment. This isn't a reason to only study high-earning fields, but it is a reason to understand the financial implications clearly before signing student loan paperwork.

Accreditation and recognition: For professional fields, check whether the program is accredited by the relevant professional body. For international students, check whether qualifications are recognized in the country where you intend to work.

Institution vs. Program: Which Matters More?

The relative importance of institution prestige versus program quality varies by field. In fields like consulting, finance, law, and academia, where hiring is heavily concentrated at specific target schools, institutional prestige matters significantly. In most other fields, what you studied and what you can demonstrate matter more than where you studied it. A well-regarded program at a second-tier school in a high-demand field will often produce better outcomes than a peripheral program at a prestigious institution.

Subject Choice: Passion vs. Pragmatism

The tension between studying what you love and studying what leads to employment is real but often overstated. The genuine guidance is nuanced: choose a subject you find genuinely interesting enough to sustain motivation through years of studying and a career of applying, but within that constraint, consider the employment outcomes and take career preparation seriously regardless of subject. A philosophy student who actively builds analytical and communication skills, networks deliberately, and targets graduate employers who value humanistic thinking will outperform a business student who coasts through a vocational program without developing genuine competence.

Postgraduate Study: When Does It Add Value?

Postgraduate degrees (master's, doctoral) add clear value when: the specific career requires them (academia, clinical psychology, some engineering specializations); you're changing fields and need credentialing; or you have a specific research interest that genuinely requires the depth of doctoral study. They add questionable value when taken immediately after undergraduate study to delay entering the job market, or when the credential isn't specifically required by target employers.

The financially optimal approach for many people: enter the workforce after undergraduate study, gain experience, and pursue postgraduate education, potentially employer-funded, when a specific need or opportunity becomes clear.
